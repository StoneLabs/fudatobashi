There's no single right way to lay out your cards. Players build very different 定位置 (fixed layouts), and this guide shows the ideas behind them so you can find your own.

# What 定位置 is

You can put your 25 cards anywhere on your side. A 定位置 (teiichi) is a layout you decide ahead of time: each card has its own spot, and you put it there every game.

Your side has three rows: the 上段 (top row) by the centre line, the 中段 (middle row), and the 下段 (bottom row) nearest you. The cards sit in a left group and a right group, with a space between them.

A 定位置 helps in two ways. You don't have to learn where your own cards are. And your hand learns the way to each spot, so when you hear a card, it already knows where to go.

# Why it saves time

Before a match, you get 15 minutes to remember all 50 cards. The other player's side is new every game, but yours doesn't have to be. With a 定位置 you know well, your side is memorised the moment it's placed.

A layout you know well is also fast to put down: all 25 cards in under a minute, without stopping to think. Then the 15 minutes are free for their side. For example, you can learn their cards in groups that start with the same sounds, then close your eyes and test yourself.

# Short and long kimariji

A card's kimariji decides how much time you have to reach it.

**One-sound cards.** The seven 一字決まり (one-sound cards: {む} {す} {め} {ふ} {さ} {ほ} {せ}) are decided by the first sound, so both players can go at once. Their spot matters for speed: your bottom row is closest to you, your top row closest to the other player. Players differ on how to group them:

- **Together.** Some keep all seven in one part of the bottom row. That gives the hand one short path to learn, but the other player can guard that one area too.
- **Spread out.** Others spread them over both sides of the bottom row. The other player can't guard them all from one spot, but there are more paths to learn.

**Long cards.** The longest are the six 大山札 (six-sound cards), which start {あさぼらけ}, {きみがため} and {わたのはら}. You hear the start well before you know which card it is, so you have time to move.

- Some players put the 大山札 at the outer edges of the middle and bottom rows, on their strong-hand side (the right for a right-handed player). They can guard them there while they wait.
- Others use long cards to fill the spots that are harder to reach, like the top row.

Kimariji get shorter as cards are read, though. A long card in a far spot can become a short one later in the match.

# Look-alike cards

友札 (look-alike cards) start with the same sounds, like {なげき} and {なげけ}. Players differ a lot on where to put them.

- **Split them.** Some players put look-alike cards far apart, for example one on each side. The other player can't go for both with one move, and the layout is harder for them to learn. But you have to wait for the deciding sound to know which way to go, and there's more to remember.
- **Keep them together.** Others keep them side by side. That's easier to remember, and you can start moving on the first sounds, then take the right card when the deciding sound comes. But the other player can also move early to the same spot.
- **A mix.** Some split a few pairs and keep the rest together, so their side stays easy to remember.

# Left, right and rows

**Left and right.** Cards on your strong-hand side are the quickest for your hand to reach.

- Some players put more cards there, along with the cards they take best.
- Others keep both sides about even, so they can move just as easily either way.

**Rows.** The top row is far from you and close to the other player. Players fill the rows in different shapes:

- **Fewest on top.** The bottom row gets the most cards and the top row the fewest. Your cards stay close to your hand.
- **Fewer in the middle.** The top and bottom rows get more. Your side is harder for the other player to remember, but more of your cards sit within their easy reach.

**The open middle.** Some players leave the gap between their left and right groups empty. Others put a card or two in the middle of the top row. A card there is easy to find, but it's far from both groups, so it's hard to cover while you guard the rest.

**If you're starting out:** one simple way to begin is to put about the same number of cards on each side, and fewer in the top row. You can shift the balance later.

# When cards move

Your side changes during a match. When you take a card from their side, you send them one of yours. This is 送り札 (sending a card). When they take one from your side, they send you one of theirs. So your 定位置 is where your side starts, not where it stays.

**Cards you receive.** Some players plan a second spot for each card, or keep a simple rule like "cards I receive go here". Then even a card sent to break up their layout has a place right away. Others put each new card wherever it fits best at the time, and practise finding cards in new spots.

**Gaps.** As your side gets smaller, gaps open up. Some players close them, moving cards down a row and evening out left and right, so their cards stay close to them. Others leave their cards where they are, so the layout they memorised stays the same. Every move means both of you have a new spot to remember.

# How fixed to be

Players don't agree on how fixed a layout should be.

- **Mostly fixed.** Some players keep one layout and change it only bit by bit. A fixed layout is quick to learn and to place, and your hand builds habits fast.
- **A base, not a rule.** Others keep a base layout but practise placing cards anywhere. Cards move around in a match anyway, and they don't want to slow down when one ends up somewhere new.
- **Almost none.** A few top players hardly use fixed places at all. They trust themselves to win from any layout.

**If you're starting out:** one simple way to begin is to keep the same layout for a few weeks, so your hand has time to learn it.

>{.tobi} Even the best players disagree here. So your ideas count too!

# Find your own

No layout is right for everyone. Yours works if you can remember it without effort and take cards from it quickly. One way to get there:

1. Pick the ideas from this guide that make sense to you.
2. Place your side again and again, saying each kimariji quietly, until it takes under a minute.
3. Test yourself. In this app's Teiichi mode, your cards turn face down after you place them, and you tap each one as its kimariji is called.
4. Play, and watch for what keeps going wrong, like a card in a spot your hand finds awkward. Change one thing at a time.

Your 定位置 can change as you get better. Now and then, try an idea you didn't pick at first, and keep what works for you.

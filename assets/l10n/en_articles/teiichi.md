Strong players put their cards in the same places every game. Here's why it helps, and how to build a layout of your own.

# What 定位置 is

You can put your 25 cards anywhere on your side. Most players decide ahead of time where each card goes, and put it there every game. That's their 定位置 (teiichi), their fixed layout.

Your side has three rows: the 上段 (top row) by the centre line, the 中段 (middle row), and the 下段 (bottom row) nearest you. Most players keep one group of cards on the left and one on the right.

A 定位置 helps in two ways. You don't have to learn where your own cards are. And your hand learns the way to each spot, so when you hear a card, it already knows where to go.

# Why it saves time

Before a match, you get 15 minutes to remember all 50 cards. The other player's side is new every game, but yours doesn't have to be. With a good 定位置, your side is memorised the moment it's placed.

A good layout is also fast to put down. You can place all 25 cards in under a minute without stopping to think. Then the 15 minutes are for their side: learn their cards in groups that start with the same sounds, close your eyes to test yourself, and go back over the ones you missed.

# Where the first cards go

Many players start from the same few habits:

- **一字決まり** (one-sound cards: む す め ふ さ ほ せ) are decided by the first sound, so many players put them in the bottom row, closest to their hand. Some spread them over both sides, so the other player can't aim at one spot for all of them.
- **Cards with long kimariji** give you more time, so many players put them at the outer edges. The longest are the six 大山札, which start あさぼらけ, きみがため and わたのはら and need six sounds. Many keep them on their strong-hand side, where they're easy to guard while you wait.
- **Your favourite cards** often go on your strong-hand side too.

# Look-alike cards

友札 (look-alike cards) start with the same sounds, like なげき and なげけ. Players disagree most about where to put them.

- **Split them.** Some players put look-alike cards far apart, often one on each side. If they sit together, the other player can go for both with one move, and learns your layout more easily.
- **Keep them together.** Others keep them side by side. It's easier to remember, and you can start moving on the first sounds and take the right card when the deciding sound comes. The downside is that the other player can move early to the same spot.
- **In between.** Many players split some pairs, but not so many that they can't remember their own side.

**If you're starting out:** keep look-alike cards together at first, and split a few once your layout feels easy.

# Left, right and rows

**Left and right.** Many players put more cards on their strong-hand side, because that hand is faster. Others keep the sides about even, so they can move just as easily either way.

**Rows.** Most players put the fewest cards in the top row, far from them and close to the other player, and the most in the bottom row. Some put fewer in the middle row instead. That's harder for the other player to remember, but more of your cards sit in the top row, where they're harder for you to take.

**The open middle.** There's usually a gap between your left and right groups. Some players put a card or two in the middle of the top row, for example one card of a look-alike pair. A card on its own is easy to find, but it's far from both groups, so it's harder to get back to.

**If you're starting out:** keep the sides about even, with fewer cards in the top row. You can change the balance later.

# When cards move

Your side changes during a match. When you take a card from their side, you send them one of yours. This is 送り札 (sending a card). When they take one from you, they send you one. So your 定位置 is where your side starts, not where it stays. Two things help:

- **Where new cards go.** Some players plan a second spot for their cards, or keep a simple rule like "cards I receive go here". Then you know where a card goes, even one sent on purpose to break up your layout.
- **Closing gaps.** As your side gets smaller, many players move cards down a row and even out left and right. This keeps their cards close to them and far from the other player.

# How strict to be

Players don't agree on how fixed a layout should be.

- **Mostly fixed.** Some clubs give beginners one shared 定位置 and let them change it bit by bit. A fixed layout is quick to learn and to place, and your hand builds habits fast.
- **A base, not a rule.** Others keep a base layout but practise placing cards anywhere. Cards move around in a match anyway, and they don't want to slow down when one ends up somewhere new.
- **Almost none.** A few top players hardly use fixed places at all. They trust themselves to win from any layout.

**If you're starting out:** keep one layout for a few weeks. When the same problem keeps coming back, like a card in a spot your hand finds awkward, change one thing at a time.

>{.tobi} Even the best players disagree here. So your ideas count too!

# Build your own

The best 定位置 is one you can remember without effort and take cards from quickly. To build yours:

1. Start with the usual habits: one-sound cards low, long kimariji at the edges, fewer cards in the top row.
2. Decide what to do with look-alike cards.
3. Place your side again and again, saying each kimariji quietly, until it takes under a minute.
4. Test yourself. In this app's 定位置 practice, your cards flip face down and you find each one as it's read.
5. Play, and change one thing at a time.
